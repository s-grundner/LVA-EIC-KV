\section{Digital Instruments and Music Theory}

Before discussing the design, a few fundamentals of music theory have to be considered and how implementing a digital instrument in silicon is sensible. The MIDI Manufactures Association defines the \ac{GM1} and its extentsion the \ac{GM2} standard to provide guidelines for developing MIDI compatible instruments. 

\subsection{The MIDI-Protocol}

The \acs{MIDI}-protocol is based on a serial asynchronous data transmission, where at most three bytes are sent in succession with a dataformat as shown in \autoref{tab:midi-frame}. It is noted, that not all commands require a second data byte which is why it is specified in brackets as an optional byte in \autoref{tab:midi-frame}. In this report all required commands provide a second data byte, therefor a length of three bytes will be assumed from now on.

The first byte combines two four-bit values representing a command and a channel number. Depending on the command code, the following databytes can be interpreted accordingly by a MIDI-sink listening on a pre-set channel. The most important and only command codes used in this project are listed in \autoref{tab:midi-spec}.

A seven bit note number (denoted as \texttt{kkkkkkk} in \autoref{tab:midi-spec}) correlates per default with the piano keys of a \ac{12ET}, where the middle C ($C_4$) is equivalent to a MIDI note number of $60$ in decimal \cite[sec~2.7.1.1]{gm2_specification_online}.

\subsection{Instrument Tuning}

A musical octave is an interval of two notes, where the frequency of the higher note is exactly twice as high as the lower note. The \acs{12ET} is a system which divides this octave into twelve notes which are referred to in \ac{SPN} as letters from $A$ to $G$ with accidentals ($\flat$, $\sharp$) and an octave number $n$ as shown in \autoref{tab:octave}.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \caption{\acs{SPN} Nomenclature for notes in a 12 tone tempered octave, where $n$ is the octave-number.}\label{tab:octave}
\begin{tabular}{c|cccccccccccc|c}
    \color{gray} $B_{n-1}$ & $C_n$ & $\dfrac{C\sharp_n}{D\flat_n}$ & $D_n$ & $\dfrac{D\sharp_n}{E\flat_n}$ & $E_n$ & $F_n$ & $\dfrac{F\sharp_n}{G\flat_n}$ & $G_n$ & $\dfrac{G\sharp_n}{A\flat_n}$ & $A_n$ & $\dfrac{A\sharp_n}{B\flat_n}$ & $B_n$ & \color{gray} $C_{n+1}$ 
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

These notes are equally tempered, meaning the frequency-ratio of two adjacent notes is constant for all musical notes. For an octave of 12 notes, this ratio equates to $\sqrt[12]{2}\approx 1.05946$, leading naturally to a logarithmic scale.

To assert actual frequency values to the notes, a pitch standard has to be decided on. \acs{GM2} suggests the ISO-16 pitch standard, where note number $69$ in decimal ($A_4$) is tuned to $\SI{440}{\hertz}$ \cite[sec~2.7.1.1]{gm2_specification_online}. With this reference frequency, the absolute frequency of all notes can be calculated by multiplying with the defined ratio, yielding \autoref{eq:f-K} for frequency $f(K)$ in dependence of the MIDI note number $K$.

\begin{equation}
    f(K) = \SI{440}{\hertz} \cdot (\!\!\sqrt[12]{2}\,)^{(K-69)} = \SI{440}{\hertz} \cdot 2^{(K-69)/12}\label{eq:f-K}
\end{equation}
